### 问题

吃苦还是舒适

> 「该让孩子过得比我们轻松，还是让他从小知道日子不总是顺的？」

### 引言

「多扛一点」和「不要白受苦」背后常常是同一个目标。有用的做法是先把「吃苦」这个词翻译成一件具体的事，否则两个人说的根本不是一回事。

### 场景

周二晚上八点前，九岁的米娅把同一段曲子第三次拉错，把琴弓放到谱架上，眼圈红红地说她不想再学小提琴了。

平时跟米娅说德语的托马斯从电脑前抬起头：“这学期的费用已经交了，我们说好让她坚持到六月底”，他语气平静。

平时跟米娅说中文的林静搂住女儿的肩膀，低声对托马斯说：“她要是不喜欢，我们就别逼她”，说着轻轻拍了拍米娅的背。

米娅看看爸爸，又看看妈妈，等着看此刻到底什么算数。

### 一句话定位

> 真正的分歧不在严还是松，而在孩子哭着说不想学的那一刻，家里先算数的是事先的约定，还是孩子此刻说的话；你们俩其实都想要一个能独立、扛得住事的孩子，也都不想羞辱他。

### 什么条件下哪种做法成立

#### 1. 刻意让他练，还是顺其自然？

- A（成立条件）：如果孩子有支持、任务也在他能力范围内，历练可以让他更强。
- B（成立条件）：如果任务超出能力，身边又没有人，那就不是教育，而是压垮。

#### 2. 该买还是该忍？

- A（成立条件）：如果预算宽裕，买这个东西也不替代共处的时间，那就没什么问题。
- B（成立条件）：如果买东西是为了补上缺席的时间或自己的愧疚，那它解决不了真正的问题。

#### 3. 几岁可以自己出门？

- A（成立条件）：如果环境安全、有人同行、并且是逐步放手，早一点是可行的。
- B（成立条件）：如果路上不安全，或孩子还没准备好，推迟不是娇惯，而是一个判断。

### 流派并排

#### 1. 先约定派

- 主张：说好的事就算兴致没了也要做完：约定的期限要走完，要不要停，走完之后再谈。
- 适用谁：事先就明确约定了期限、并且约定时孩子在场的家庭。

#### 2. 交还选择权派

- 主张：孩子应该能一起决定要不要继续；他说“不想”，首先是一条关于孩子的信息，不是不听话。
- 适用谁：孩子已经能说出自己的理由、平时也较早参与决定自己时间的家庭。

### 自检三问

1. 你们家是谁约定的“要坚持多久”？约定的时候孩子在场吗？
2. 平时孩子说“不想了”，在你们家意味着什么：常常这样、很少这样，还是只有这一件事？

### The question

Hardship or comfort

> “Should our child have it easier than we did — or is that exactly how they learn that not everything is easy?”

### Introduction

Behind “being able to take more” and “no unnecessary suffering” there is often the same goal. It helps to translate the word “hardship” into one concrete situation first — otherwise each of you is talking about something different.

### The scene

Tuesday evening, just before eight: nine-year-old Mia plays the same passage wrong on her violin for the third time, lays the bow on the music stand and says, eyes wet, that she doesn’t want to learn the violin any more.

“The term is paid for, and we agreed you would stick it out until the end of June,” says Thomas, who speaks German with Mia, looking up calmly from his laptop.

“If she doesn’t like it, we shouldn’t force her,” says Jing, who speaks Chinese with Mia, quietly to Thomas, and puts an arm around her daughter’s shoulders.

Mia looks from one to the other, waiting to see what counts now.

### What this is really about

> The real question is not strict versus lenient but what counts first when the child is in tears, the agreement made beforehand or what she says right now – although you both want a child who becomes independent and can endure things, and neither of you wants to shame her.

### Two perspectives (no winner)

_The points below are editorial conversation prompts (grade D), not statements of fact, and not a claim about parents of any origin or culture._

#### Perspective A — Wants to protect: Independence and resilience

- I want our child to be able to take something and not be picked up at the first hurdle. [Grade D · Conversation prompt]
- Much of what I learned myself, I learned because it was not comfortable. [Grade D · Conversation prompt]

#### Perspective B — Wants to protect: Support, and a no to unnecessary suffering

- I want to tell the difference between a challenge our child grows through and a burden they are left alone with. [Grade D · Conversation prompt]
- I want to be sure that we never confuse strictness with shaming. [Grade D · Conversation prompt]

### Shared goal

- Both of us want a child who becomes independent and can take something.
- Both of us reject corporal punishment and shaming — in Germany it is also a right of the child.
- Both of us believe that effort is worth it.

### When each position holds

#### 1. Deliberately demanding — or simply letting it run?

- A (holds if): If the child has support and the task can be managed, effort can strengthen.
- B (holds if): If the task is beyond their strength and nobody is standing by, it is no longer upbringing but overload.

#### 2. Buying something — or deliberately going without?

- A (holds if): If the budget allows it and the purchase does not replace shared time, there is nothing wrong with it.
- B (holds if): If something is bought to make up for missing time or a bad conscience, it does not solve the actual issue.

#### 3. Out on their own — from when?

- A (holds if): If the surroundings are safe, someone goes along and you let go step by step, earlier is possible.
- B (holds if): If the route is unsafe or the child is not there yet, postponing is not spoiling but an assessment.

### Schools of thought side by side

#### 1. Agreement first

- Core claim: A commitment counts even when the enthusiasm fades: the agreed period is finished first, and you talk about stopping afterwards.
- Fits whom: Families that agreed in advance how long something runs, with the child present when it was agreed.

#### 2. The child’s choice first

- Core claim: The child should help decide whether to continue; a “no” is first a piece of information about the child, not disobedience.
- Fits whom: Families whose child can already name reasons and takes part early in decisions about their own free time.

#### 3. Grit as a strength

- Core claim: Perseverance combined with passion for a goal that matters is a strength that can grow.
- Representative book: Angela Duckworth: “Grit”
- Fits whom: Families whose child has a goal of their own that they want to stick with.

### Real disputes (not adjudicated)

#### 1. The agreement first, or the child’s choice first?

- One side: Having said yes, you finish: an agreed period holds even when the enthusiasm is gone, and stopping is discussed afterwards.
- The other side: When the child clearly says no, the child should help decide whether to continue; a no is first of all information, not disobedience.
- The laws collected here do not say whether a class has to run to the end of the term; the BGB (section 1626(2)) only says parents discuss questions of parental care with the child as far as the child’s stage of development allows.

#### 2. Can “sticking with it” itself be trained?

- One side: Yes: passion and perseverance for a goal that matters can grow (as stated on Duckworth’s own book page).
- The other side: Not necessarily: a synthesis of 88 samples says programmes to enhance grit may have only weak effects, and grit was very strongly tied to conscientiousness there.
- Most samples in the synthesis are not small children, and Duckworth’s page says nothing about hardship imposed by others; in neither source did we read anything about children’s music lessons, and the laws collected here do not address the question.

#### 3. What do you hear when the child says “I don’t want to”?

- One side: If the task is within reach and someone sits beside the child, staying with it can make the child stronger, so tears are first a moment to accompany.
- The other side: If the task exceeds the child’s strength and nobody stands by, it is no longer upbringing but overload, so tears are first a signal to check the task.
- Both sides talk about the same condition (manageable, with support); the disagreement is how you judge which one applies to your child right now, and the laws collected here give no yardstick. If the strain worries you physically or emotionally, a paediatrician is the first place to turn.

#### Remo H. Largo (paediatrician, Children's Hospital Zurich; 1943–2020) — "Kinderjahre" – we read only the publisher's description

- According to the publisher, bringing up a child means for him: supporting the child's development so that it can become the being it has the makings of.
- He asks about disposition and environment and about the child's basic needs and abilities.
- The publisher's text stresses the importance of maturation and of self-determined experiences.
- Limits: We read only the publisher's book description, not the book. It says nothing explicit about “toughness” or “spoiling”; the book serves here only as a pointer to start from the individual child.

#### Angela Duckworth (psychologist) — "Grit" – we read her own book page

- Her claim: outstanding achievement rests not on talent but on a blend of passion and persistence she calls “grit”.
- Grit, for her, is passion and perseverance combined for one singularly important goal.
- She writes that she has found scientific evidence that grit can grow.
- Limits: We read the author's website, not the book – she is presenting her own concept there. The synthesis of 88 samples listed above found only a moderate link with performance. And: passion is part of Duckworth's grit. The page does not talk about hardship imposed by others.
- What we read: [Angela Duckworth: “Grit” (own website)](https://www.angeladuckworth.com/grit)

### Pre-written demo Q&A (not a live AI, not evidence)

- Question: My husband wants our daughter (7) to walk to school in winter, even in the rain – he says it toughens her up. I'd rather drive her. Am I spoiling her?
- Answer: The word “spoiling” does not appear in the sources collected here, and none says whether a walk to school in the rain is good for a particular child. What they do say: German law requires parents to take account of the child's growing ability and need to act independently – and to discuss matters with the child as far as fits her stage of development. The Chinese law names perseverance through hardship as an explicit aim, but speaks of work within the child's capacity, and of strictness together with kindness. So you and your husband are both within what these texts describe. Translate “toughening up” into something concrete: which route, how far, with whom, and in what weather not? And ask your daughter what she herself would like to manage. The line both laws draw is a different one: no violence, no humiliation. This is a collection of public texts, not advice for your child.
