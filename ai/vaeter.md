### 问题

到底是谁在带孩子

> 「一个说：我已经比大多数爸爸做得多了。另一个说：这个家的待办清单全装在我脑子里。谁有理？」

### 引言

这一节不是两个国家的比较。这件事两边都没解决：中文里有专门形容父亲长期缺位的说法，德国的官方统计也摆在那里。放数字在这里，是为了把争吵从「你这个人」移到「这套安排」上。

### 场景

九点刚过，乔纳斯加完班，开门进屋，一脸疲惫。米拉终于睡着了，静从接孩子、做晚饭、洗澡，到读完两本绘本，这会儿才刚给自己泡上一杯茶。

乔纳斯先开口：「昨天是我洗的澡，上周六我陪米拉在游乐场待了一上午。」静把幼儿园的条子推到他面前：明天要带疫苗本，米拉的雨靴也小了，这两件事，从周二起就只有她的脑子记着。

乔纳斯把条子看了两遍，这时儿童房里，米拉半梦半醒地喊了一声「妈妈」。

### 一句话定位

> 争的不是谁更努力，而是一个家怎么看待这些活：只算动手的时间，还是连惦记也算；是各人有空时搭把手，还是每一项都明确归到某个人名下。

### 共同目标

- 两边的文化都没解决这件事——谁也没有资格教育谁。
- 两个人都不是在偷懒，争的是同一件稀缺品：时间。
- 两个人都认同爸爸多参与是好事。分歧在怎么换、换多少，不在要不要。

### 什么条件下哪种做法成立

#### 1. 「我已经比平均做得多了」，这够吗？

- A（成立条件）：作为一句对外比较的事实陈述，它是成立的——平均水平确实不高。
- B（成立条件）：但对于「这周谁四点去接」，平均值帮不上忙，只有具体安排能解决。

#### 2. 先在家里重新分，还是先改外面的条件？

- A（成立条件）：只要家里还有余量（晚上、周末、可以外包的家务），先在家里谈会更快。
- B（成立条件）：如果余量已经用尽，卡在工作时间或托位上，继续互相消耗没有用，该谈的是对外的安排。

#### 3. 算小时，还是算「谁在操心」？

- A（成立条件）：按小时算，分工很快能写下来。
- B（成立条件）：按操心算，就得先把那张看不见的清单写出来，否则总有一个人觉得自己一直没被看见。

### 流派并排

#### 1. 明确分工派：把活列成清单、逐项认领

- 主张：孩子和伴侣的每一件待办都要记在脑子里，本身就是一份实实在在的负担；据出版社介绍，出路是把「谁负责」分公平、学会放手、留出空当。

#### 3. 隔代支持派：把祖辈算进分工

- 主张：这一派把祖辈出手带娃看作一种现实的办法，应当算进家里的分工里，同时提前说清祖辈管哪些事、教养上谁拿主意。

### 真分歧（不裁决）

#### 1. 是「没时间」，还是「没分到」

- 一方：很晚才到家的那个人，时间确实被工作、通勤、轮班占满了，这不是想不想做的问题。
- 另一方：有没有人担，也要看活有没有归到谁名下：没有明确归属的事，有空时才有人做，其余时候就落在脑子里记着它的那个人身上。

- 一方：祖辈搭把手，让两位家长都有余力，这是减负，不是躲开。
- 另一方：祖辈接走的那部分，夫妻之间就不必再重新分了，分工也可能因此一直没被谈开。

#### 3. 分工要不要写成清单

- 一方：清单让人看见谁认领了什么，不用再凭印象吵。

### 自检三问

1. 回想你们上一个差点吵起来的晚上：当时缺的是时间，还是那件事本来就没有人认领？
2. 家里有哪些事，是只有一个人记得——什么时候该预约、该买、该带？如果这个人整整一个星期不去想，谁会先发现？
3. 除了你们两个，现在还有谁在分担日常（祖辈、邻居、托管）？他们做的事，哪些你们当作帮忙，哪些其实是本该你们两个之间分的活，你们说开过吗？

Diese Szene ist kein Vergleich zwischen zwei Ländern. Gelöst hat das Thema keine der beiden Seiten: Im Chinesischen gibt es ein eigenes Schlagwort für den dauerhaft abwesenden Vater, und in Deutschland sagt die amtliche Statistik das Ihre. ### Die Szene

Kurz nach neun schließt Jonas die Wohnungstür auf, müde nach einem Tag mit Überstunden. Mila schläft endlich, und Jing hat nach Abholen, Abendessen, Baden und zwei Bilderbüchern gerade erst den Tee aufgesetzt.

## English

### The question

Who is actually doing the caring?

> “One says: I do more than most fathers. The other says: I have the whole list in my head. Who is right?”

### Introduction

This scene is not a comparison between two countries. Neither side has solved the issue: Chinese has its own catchword for the permanently absent father, and in Germany the official statistics say their part. The figures are here to move the argument away from the person and towards the division of work.

### The scene

Just after nine, Jonas unlocks the front door, worn out after a day of overtime. Mila is finally asleep, and Jing, after pick-up, dinner, bath and two picture books, has only just put the kettle on.

“I did the bath yesterday,” Jonas says, “and on Saturday I was at the playground with Mila all morning.” Jing slides the daycare's note across the table: “The vaccination booklet has to go in tomorrow, and Mila's rain boots are too small – and since Tuesday that has lived only in my head.”

Jonas reads the note twice, and from the children's room Mila calls out “Mama,” half asleep.

### What this is really about

> The argument is not about who tries harder but about how a family sees this work: do only the hours of hands-on tasks count, or the thinking ahead too – and does everyone pitch in when there is time, or does each task explicitly belong to someone?

### Two perspectives (no winner)

#### Perspective B — Wants to protect: The invisible list in the head

- I do not only do the tasks, I also keep everything in mind: the vaccination appointment, the clothing size, the present for the children’s birthday party. [Grade D · Conversation prompt]
- I want to be able to hand a task over completely — including the thinking about it, not just carrying it out. [Grade D · Conversation prompt]

### Shared goal

- Neither side has solved this topic — nobody here is in a position to lecture the other.
- Neither of us is lazy. The argument is about the same scarce good: time.
- Both of us think it is good when the father is more present. The argument is about how and how much is swapped — not whether.

### When each position holds

#### 1. “I do more than average” — is that enough?

- A (holds if): As a statement about the comparison with the outside world, that is true; the average really is low.
- B (holds if): For the question of who picks up at 4 p.m. this week, an average does not help. Only a concrete plan helps there.

#### 2. Divide things up at home first — or change something outside first?

- A (holds if): As long as there is still room at home (evenings, the weekend, handing tasks over), you will get further in the conversation faster.
- B (holds if): If that room is used up and it comes down to working hours or a childcare place, you are only wearing each other out. Then the conversation belongs outside.

#### 3. Count hours — or count the thinking?

- A (holds if): If you count in hours, a division is quickly written down.
- B (holds if): If you count the thinking, you first have to make the invisible list visible — otherwise one person feels permanently overlooked.

### Schools of thought side by side

#### 1. Spell it out: list the tasks and assign them

- Core claim: The unpaid, often invisible work is turned into a list of individual tasks; then you settle who takes the lead on each one.
- Representative book: Eve Rodsky, “Fair Play” (we read only the publisher's description)
- Fits whom: Couples who would rather have it in writing who does what and are willing to sit down for an evening to do it.

#### 2. Mental load: count the thinking too

- Core claim: Keeping every to-do of children and partner in your head is a real burden; the ways out, according to the publisher, are fairer responsibilities, letting go of tasks and creating free space.
- Representative book: Patricia Cammarata, “Raus aus der Mental Load-Falle” (we read only the publisher's description)
- Fits whom: Families where the tasks get done but one person has to keep track of everything.

#### 3. Bring the grandparents in

- Core claim: This view sees the grandparents' help as a practical solution that belongs in the division of work – provided it is said out loud beforehand what they take on and who has the last word on how the child is raised.
- Fits whom: Families with grandparents nearby or living in the household, where two parents need more hands anyway.

### Real disputes (not adjudicated)

#### 1. No time – or nothing assigned?

- One side: Someone who gets home late really does have fewer hours; working hours, commuting and shifts cannot simply be thought away.
- The other side: Whether someone carries a share is also a question of assignment: what belongs to no one by name gets done when there is time – and otherwise stays with whoever has it in their head.
- The official figures on this page are averages (women do 1 hour 19 minutes more unpaid work a day; fathers plan 3.8 months of parental allowance, mothers 14.9). They cannot tell you which of the two applies in your home.

#### 2. Grandparents: help or substitute?

- One side: When grandma and grandpa pitch in, both parents have more room to breathe – that is relief, not avoidance.
- The other side: What the grandparents take over no longer has to be redistributed between the parents, so the division of work can stay unspoken.
- The official sources on this page say nothing on this; our records hold only everyday-practice accounts (grade D), no checked study.

#### 3. Write the division down as a list?

- One side: A list makes visible who has taken what and replaces arguing from memory.
- The other side: A list records only what is already visible; anticipating and the unplanned have to be put into words before they can get onto it.
- There is no official recommendation on this; the Familienportal only sets out rules for parental allowance, not how you divide things at home.

### Three self-check questions

1. Think of the last evening it got tense between you: was the problem a lack of time – or that the task in question had no clear owner?
2. Which things in your home does only one of you keep in mind – appointments, sizes, presents – and who would notice if that person stopped thinking about them for a week?
3. Besides the two of you, who carries something in everyday life right now (grandparents, neighbours, childcare), and have you ever said out loud which of it is help and which is really a task that belongs between the two of you?

### Questions for the couple

1. Let us write down for one week what actually comes up in childcare and housework — not estimated, but noted. Do we see the same list afterwards?
